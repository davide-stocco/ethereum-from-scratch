## The thesis this page exists to argue

The site's first page says **nobody is in charge**, and then every page after it assumes that's settled. It isn't. The rules *do* change — roughly twenty times since 2015 — so somebody is deciding something, and the interesting question is what kind of power that is when it isn't authority.

**The answer: it's the power to propose, and the power to refuse.** Nobody can make the network adopt a change. Anybody running a node can decline one. Everything else follows from that asymmetry.

## Questions this page should answer

- If nobody is in charge, how did twenty upgrades happen? (A process with no vote: proposals, argument, implementation by independent teams, and finally node operators choosing to run the new software — or not.)
- What is an **EIP**, as a social object rather than a document? (A standing invitation to be argued with. Worth connecting to the [EIPs cited throughout this site](../about/forks.md) — the reader has been reading governance output all along.)
- Where does real influence sit? (Client teams, who decide what the software does; researchers, who shape what's considered; large stakers and infrastructure providers, who can decline in ways that matter. None of them *rule*, and the distinction is the page's substance.)
- What happens when agreement fails? (The chain splits. The DAO fork in 2016 is the worked example, already described on [losing your keys](../wallets/losing-your-keys.md) — and Ethereum Classic still runs the original rules, which is what "refusing" looks like in practice.)
- So what are the limits on change? (Not technical. The constraint is the cost of coordinating thousands of independent parties, plus the belief that acting arbitrarily destroys the property that gives the network value. **Social, not technical** — the conclusion the losing-your-keys page reaches from the other direction.)
- Could the supply be changed? Could balances be edited? (Yes, in the sense that a fork can do anything. No, in the sense that getting agreement is the whole difficulty — and the one time it was tried at scale it split the chain permanently.)
- How does this compare to governance people already know? (Standards bodies, central bank independence, open-source maintainership. The closest honest analogue, and where the analogy breaks.)
