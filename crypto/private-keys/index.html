    Private keys

    Permanent. 256-bit keys on the secp256k1 curve, unchanged since Ethereum launched in 2015.

    A private key is a number. That's the whole of it.

    Private key

    A randomly chosen integer, 256 bits long — which from hex and bytes you'll recognise as 32 bytes, or 64 hex characters.

    Whoever knows it can produce signatures for the matching address, and that is the whole of what owning ether consists of.

    Not a file, not a password, not a certificate issued by anyone. Nobody assigns it to you and no record exists of it anywhere.

    Try it: make one

    Press it a few times. Nothing was registered, nobody was asked, no server was contacted — your browser picked a random number. That is the entire process, and it's the first genuinely strange thing about Ethereum: an account is not issued to you, it's a number you happened to choose.

    Never send anything to a key from this page

    To be precise about the risk, because it's easy to overstate and easy to understate.

    Nothing is at risk right now. The key you just generated controls an account that has never been used and holds nothing. There is nothing to steal.

    The danger is only if you ever put value behind it. And it's absolute, because of something worth stating plainly: the private key is the complete secret. There is nothing above it, beneath it, or alongside it — no password, no second factor, no account to lock. Anyone holding that number can sign for that address, and the network cannot tell them from you.

    This key has been generated in a browser and displayed on a screen, in a tab that is probably in your history, on a page any installed extension could read. That's fine for looking at. It is permanently unfit for holding anything, and no amount of later care fixes it.

    For real value, use wallet software that generates a key and never shows it to anyone, including you.

    How is one number enough?

    A natural objection: if a key is just a number, surely someone can guess it?

    They can't, and the reason is the size of the number. Private keys run up to about 2256:

    2^256  =  115,792,089,237,316,195,423,570,985,008,
          687,907,853,269,984,665,640,564,039,457,
          584,007,913,129,639,936

    Roughly 1077. Written out, it's 78 digits. That number resists intuition completely, so rather than reach for an analogy, here's a calculation you can run yourself.

    Try it: how long to guess a key?

    Suppose every person alive were hunting for your key, each testing keys as fast as a modern machine can manage, for the entire history of the universe.

    The defaults are 8 billion people × a billion keys per second each (8 × 1018), running for 13.8 billion years — the age of the universe.

    The result is around 1 in 1040. Not "difficult". Not "takes a long time". After exhausting the universe's entire history with every human being and every machine, they would have searched a fraction of the keyspace too small to write comfortably.

    Try making the numbers absurd — a trillion keys per second, a trillion years. It barely moves.

    This is why a key needs no protection beyond secrecy. There's no account to lock, no rate limit to enforce, nobody to appeal to. The number itself is the security, and guessing is not a strategy.

    The valid range

    Almost any 256-bit number works, but not quite all of them. A private key must be at least 1 and less than a specific constant called the order of the curve, written n:

    n = 0xFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFEBAAEDCE6AF48A03BBFD25E8CD0364141

    That's defined by secp256k1, the elliptic curve Ethereum uses (SECG, 2010) — the same one Bitcoin uses. Keys have to fall below it because of how the public key is derived; the arithmetic happens in a group with exactly n elements, so numbers at or above n simply wrap around to ones below it.

    How much of the range is excluded? Almost none.

    The gap between n and 2256 is:

    2^256 - n  =  432,420,386,565,659,656,852,420,866,394,968,145,599

    Which looks enormous until you compare it to the space it sits in. The proportion of 256-bit numbers that are too large to be valid keys is about:

    3.7 × 10^-39      —  roughly 1 in 2.7 × 10^38

    So if you pick a random 256-bit number, the chance it's out of range is far smaller than the chance of guessing someone's key. Real implementations do check, and loop if the draw fails, but the branch essentially never runs.

    This is also why "a private key is a random 256-bit number" is a perfectly good working definition, even though it's not exactly true.

    What it's for

    A private key does exactly one useful thing: it produces signatures.

    A signature is a way of proving you know a secret without telling anyone the secret.

    That sounds impossible, and with an ordinary password it is. If you prove you know a password by typing it in, whoever is on the other end now knows it too — you've handed over the very thing you were proving you had. Every proof costs you the secret.

    A signature escapes that. Give your private key a message — say, "send 1 ether to Anna" — and it computes a value with two properties:

    • Anyone can check that the value could only have been produced by someone holding the key for a particular address.
    • The value reveals nothing about the key. You can publish a thousand signatures and the key stays secret.

    And a third property, which matters just as much: the signature is bound to that one message. Change a single byte — the amount, the recipient — and it stops checking out. So a signature isn't a reusable token the way a password is. It authorises exactly one thing, once.

    It's not much like a handwritten signature

    The word is unhelpful, because the everyday version works the opposite way.

    Your handwritten signature is the same every time, which is precisely its weakness: anyone who has seen it can attempt a copy, and a copy works on any document.

    A digital signature is different for every message. Signing two transactions gives two unrelated values, and neither is any use on a third. So there's nothing to copy. Seeing a million of someone's signatures tells you nothing you could use.

    Closer analogies are all imperfect, but the useful intuition is a lock that produces a different, publicly-checkable receipt for each thing you lock — rather than a stamp that leaves the same mark.

    Transaction signing on Ethereum has worked this way with secp256k1 keys since the beginning (Wood, 2014). How the computation actually works is the subject of signatures; for now, only the properties matter.

    That's the mechanism behind every claim of ownership on Ethereum. Which leads to a definition worth stating bluntly, because it surprises people:

    "Owning" ether means knowing a number.

    Nothing in the state records that an account belongs to you. The chain holds only a balance against an address, entirely public, visible to everybody. What distinguishes you from everyone else looking at it is that you can produce valid signatures for it and they can't.

    And the key is the whole secret, not one factor among several. Nothing else is checked: no password, no second device, no identity. A signature is valid or it isn't, and producing a valid one requires only the number.

    So there's nothing to steal except the number — and nothing to lose except the number, which is the subject of what losing your keys means.

    What comes next

    Two things follow from the key, in order:

    1. The key is multiplied into a public key — easy to compute forwards, infeasible to reverse.
    2. The public key is hashed into an address.

    Each step is one-way, which is why an address reveals nothing about the key behind it.

    Seed phrases are not this

    If you've used a wallet, you've probably met twelve or twenty-four words rather than a 64-character number, and may reasonably assume that's the private key.

    It isn't. Those words are a seed, and a repeatable procedure turns one seed into many private keys.

    The direction matters, because it's easy to get backwards. The seed sits upstream of your keys, not above them as some higher authority:

    seed  ──►  private key  ──►  address

    So a seed is a convenient way to produce and re-produce keys — which is why writing down twelve words can restore a whole wallet. But it is not a second secret guarding them. Once a private key exists, it stands alone: whoever has it can sign, and the seed is irrelevant to that.

    Ethereum itself has never heard of seed phrases. They're a wallet convention, and the protocol sees only keys and signatures. Covered under wallets and seed phrases.

    Recap

    • A private key is a random 256-bit number. Nothing more.
    • Nobody issues it. You pick it, and the network never needs to know you did.
    • There are about 1077 possibilities, which is why guessing is not a strategy — every human searching for the age of the universe covers around 1 in 1040 of the space.
    • It must be below the curve order n, which excludes about 1 in 1038 of all 256-bit numbers — so in practice, any random number will do.
    • Its one job is producing signatures, and therefore "owning" ether means knowing a number.
